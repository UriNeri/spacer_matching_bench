Reviewer: 1

Comments to the Author
The manuscript by Neri et al presents a benchmarking of different tools for detecting CRISPR spacer-protospacer pairs. CRISPR-based mapping has become a popular method to predict the hosts of phages and other mobile genetic elements and to track phage-bacteria dynamics. Therefore, the guidelines provided by this study could be helpful to researchers working on microbial and phage ecology.

Although I find the topic timely and relevant, I felt a bit disappointed by the lack of ambition of this work. The benchmarking is quite basic, not taking into account key factors such as database incompleteness, compositional bias, or specificity against false positives. Furthermore, the study does not provide any general insights into the formal underpinnings for the observed trends. Therefore, its scope and applicability are reduced to a limited set of tools, versions, and parameter settings.

Major concerns:

- The study felt like a missed opportunity to study how realistic scenarios and biased datasets affect the detection of spacer-protospacer pairs. Although the authors introduce a synthetic dataset to "allow testing rare or yet-unobserved scenarios", none of those (not even relatively simple but non-uniform scenarios) were actually tested. In fact, the statistical properties of the synthetic dataset are very simplistic and far from representing real databases. The authors insist that the synthetic dataset is limited because of its small size. However, that is a very minor point compared to the lack of other realistic properties (heterogeneous contig length, compositional skewness, incompleteness...) For example, incompleteness could be relevant because some read alignment tools are more likely to produce spurious mappings if there is not a perfect matching sequence in the database.

- Limitations of the real dataset: The positive set for the real dataset is just the union of the protospacers identified by any of the tools. Thus, protospacers that are missed by all tools are completely ignored when estimating the recall. The study does not provide any estimate of how frequent those cases could be, but it would be naive to expect that tools are so complementary in their approaches that all pairs would be captured by one or another. In consequence, the reported recall overestimates the "real" recall. This is a major limitation whose effects should be assessed or at least discussed.

- I missed a more honest treatment of false positives. If you make a synthetic dataset, select pre-planned insertion points and then find out that the dataset, which is completely random (no sequence conservation, shared ancestry, or HGT), contains other identical or nearly identical sequences, then those can only be considered false positives. The authors even call these "spurious aligments", which is quite revealing of what they actually are (or what they are not: meaningful or informative). The fact that the number of those spurious alignments skyrockets with the mismatch threshold is also quite revealing: the greater the number of accepted mismatches, the greater the risk of getting "protospacers" that are not meaningful. The fraction of such hits in the synthetic dataset is quite remarkable (>10%) if one allows up to 3 mismatches. The fraction in a more realistic (synthetic) dataset with compositional bias would be even higher (because biases reduce the total entropy). From a biological point of view, such meaningless hits are false positives and should be treated as such. Thus, I was puzzled that the authors not only did not leverage the synthetic dataset to assess the specificity of different tools against false positives (which perhaps could make blastn-short perform better than bowtie1) but also counted them as true positives. I agree that doing that with the real dataset is complicated, because deciding whether a hit is meaningful or spurious would require further analyses of phylogeny and genomic context. But not doing that with the synthetic dataset seems harder to justify. In the end, finding spacer-protospacer pairs with mismatches comes at the cost of getting more false positives. Therefore, depending on the application, better performance finding pairs with 2-3 mismatches may not be that advantageous.

- Related to the previous point, the study would very much benefit from considering that users may be interested in different regions of the sensitivity vs specificity (or recall vs precision) trade-off. Currently, the manuscript only deals with recall: the best tool is the one that retrieves more hits with or without mismatches. But that is only helpful if one is willing to work at the high recall-low precision region of the trade-off. The tool recommendation would be different for users that want to work at the other end, where precision is prioritized and only exact matches are desired. Yet, the most interesting case probably lies in the middle: what happens when there is a mix of candidate protospacers with different number of mismatches in the dataset? How do different tools deal with such cases? For many applications, the best tool in that scenario would be the one that returns the most similar spacer-protospacer pair. (Or one could also argue that the best tool would be the one that returns all candidates, and then the user can decide how to prioritize them, but the most simiar pair should still be among the returned candidates.) Besides a general discussion, it would be very informative if tool comparisons were extended to such mixed scenarios.

- As the authors acknowledge, the usefulness of these results is limited to the tools and versions tested. In the introduction, they discuss some examples of technical features that produce differences in performance among tools. I wonder if it would be possible to get some insight that connected the heuristics and design features of different methods with their performance. Developing some intuition about which kind of approaches work better for this particular problem would help generalize, project, and design better methods in the future. For example, is there any design feature that explains the drop in recall when the number of targets in the database is too large? Adding more information to Table 1 would be helpful too (for example, categorizing tools based on their original purpose, exhaustive or approximate search algorithm, computational requirements...)

Minor points:

- The use of the IMG/VR4 dataset is justified by the existence of a previous spacer-protospacer mapping, which would "allow for a direct comparison to historical results". Was that comparison ever made? I could not find any further mention to that in the manuscript.

- The description of the synthetic dataset in the Methods section is insufficient. It does not become clear until the end of the manuscript that it is just a set of random sequences drawn from uniform length and compositional distributions.

- The authors say that they used the Levenshtein (or edit) distance to measure mismatches. Then they explain that they realigned spacers and protospacers using parasail with the nuc44 scoring matrix and certain penalties for gap opening and extension, and calculated the number of mismatches from such alignments. Calling that a Levenshtein distance is misleading. The Levenshtein distance implicitly embodies an alignment criterion, which amounts to minimizing such distance. Using a different criterion to align the sequences and then state that the number of mismatches equals the Levenshtein distance is simply incorrect (although in simple cases without much divergence or indels both values probably coincide).

- In the study limitations, the authors seem to imply that the distance metrics used (Levenshtein distance) only allows working with substitutions. That is not correct: the Levenshtein distance can be applied to pairs sequences with indels. If the authors decided to not study indels because they are less frequent than substitutions, that is totally fine, but it should be stated that way.

- The authors highlight the drop in performance at high target multiplicity as the most relevant finding. However, for the purpose of identifying MGE-host pairs, spacers with >1000 targets are probably not very informative (unless the MGE database is extremely redundant). Most tools work well in the 1-1000 multiplicity range, which is probably fine for most applications.

- The terrible performance of minimap2 (zero recall) is quite suspicious. Have the authors tried to lower the chaining score threshold (option -m)? With the default value (-m 40) no pairs will be found for spacers shorter than 40nt. More tuning may be necessary, but that would be a good starting point.

- The extensive discussion on the caveats that affect prediction of MGE-host pairs from spacer-protospacer pairs seems a bit off-topic. As the authors acknowledge, tools especially designed for that purpose often implement more sophisticated approaches and are not tested here. A warning note reminding the reader that spacer-protospacer matches do not necessarily imply an MGE-host relationship may be sufficient. On the other side, I miss a more explicit discussion on the reliability (or lack thereof) of imperfect matches, which could just represent spurious coincidences. I also miss some specific thoughts on why some methods perform better than others: Which internal heuristics work best? Which ones shoud be avoided? How could we improve future methods for protospacer detection?

- It would be helpful to explain how false negatives were identified in the real dataset. It can be inferred that those are spacers that have a matching protospacer in the database (based on other tools) but that is not detected by the focal tool. Nevertheless, an explicit clarification would be good. Same for the positive set: the way it is defined is a bit confusing.

- The "mismatch threshold" (section 4.1) is not explicitly defined.

- Why weren't the results of the synthetic presented in section 4.2? It seems like the simulated datased could be a good way of testing the effect of high target multiplicity. However, the results presented in the Supplementary data are confusing. Why does the drop occur at much lower multiplicities? Also, what do Fig 2 and S5 show, the "contig dependent" or "contig independent" recall?

- In the caption of Figure 2, the "detection fraction" is not properly defined. The text says "The detection fraction is the mean defection fraction..." which is a circular definition.

- Page 9, first paragraph: the text refers to Fig 2 when describing results for perfect matches. However, Fig 2 does not show separate results for 0 mismatches, only for 1 or less mismatches.

Typos and formatting issues:

- The colors and symbols assigned to each method are different in Fig 1 and 2. That is quite confusing.

- Numbers in Fig 3 are hard to read. Rounding them and using abbreviated order of magnitude preffixes (K, M, G...) would help with readability. Using log-scaling for the colormap may also be a good idea.

- Section 4.2 header: where it says "query (spacer)", shouldn't it be "target" or protospacer?

- The section headers in the Methods are off. Section 3.2 is titled "Data generation and acquisition" and only contains a table, while 3.3 and 3.4 contain the actual information about data generation. Section 3.7 has a header ("Benchmarking framework") but no content.

Reviewer: 2

Comments to the Author
The authors present an manuscript ‘Tool Choice drastically Impacts CRISPR Spacer-Protospacer Detection’ in which they compare different computational tools in their ability to detect putative Spacer-Protospacer using a large set of spacers and metavirome data and a simulated set. I think this is an interesting and useful comparison but the analysis done and presentation are somewhat preliminary. A major revision would likely be publishable.
Major points:
1. Title: Should reflect computational nature of study and purposes of detection. It should use a more appropriate word than drastic.
2. Introduction. Most of these points have been examined using bioinformatic and experimental tools before and these should be cited. Reference and comment on the basis of current tools as well as older versions and reviews.
3. Cite DUST if it has been used in this type of application before. Shannan entropy is not important here, base pairing is.  Cite where it has been used in this type of analysis.
4. The blast issues reported in 2018 appear to be resolved with later bug fixes and better documentation. They need not be reiterated here, unless discussing specific current issues or parameters.
5. Blastn. The blastn results are most relevant to many current tools and analyses. There should be more analysis of blastn’s performance.  In this study blastn parameters used are -max_target_seqs 1000000 -evalue 1e-5 -task blastn-short. The effect of these should be considered on the results, particularly on those in Fig 2. I don't believe they will change the result but it should be tested.

These parameters could be changed by users, or in future versions of blastn. Therefore the parameters being used in this benchmarking, by default in the version of blastn-short being used, should be stated (e.g. match, mismatch. etc)

I note that some spacer matching tools e.g. CRISPRTarget use unusual blastn parameters for matching, albeit for a different purpose and on a smaller scale (https://crispr.otago.ac.nz/CRISPRTarget/crispr_analysis.html) some other sets of  parameters should also be tested e.g . wordsize if not as small as possible (7) or -evalue.

6. Figures, some lines and shapes are difficult to distinguish and these should be checked throughout e.g. Figure 2. its difficult to distinguish lines for Bowtie1 and Bowtie2. Figure 3, can’t read white on blue. The supplementary figures should have better legends and be tidied up e.g. ‘percision’ typo in S3, yellow coloured lines that are difficult to see in Figure S4.
7. A major point is that abundance of the target in the databases affects recall when it is high (Figure 2). This could be affected (i) parameters e.g. -max_target_seqs 1000000 (as noted above) for blastn, do any of the other tools have similar perhaps default parameters? (ii) by (i) the filtering of repeated or low complexity regions e.g. using DUST. The effect of DUST should be tested, are the sequences with high abundance being missed of low complexity , or perhaps represent a certain type of repeat (common sequence motif) that might not be biologically relevant. These ideas should be further examined, and discussed. They are discussed briefly in the current discussion.
8. The authors state they provide general guidelines in the abstract but these are less clear in the discussion. These should be clarified, if they suggest using bowtie1 they should say so, and any consequences in terms of speed or resources compared to blastn. Are there use cases in which other tools might be better? e.g. when > 3 mismatches need to be detected as when looking for priming spacer matches, or self targeting?
9. This study focuses only on metagenomic data and is useful as such. It would be useful to see a set of spacers from reference genomes, and known prokaryotic viruses. Some of the effects seen here could be due to biases in the spacer library or IMGVR datasets.
